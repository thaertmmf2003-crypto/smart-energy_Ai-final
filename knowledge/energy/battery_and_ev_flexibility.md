# Battery storage and EV charging flexibility

## Battery storage for peak shaving
An on-site battery can discharge during peak periods to reduce grid import and
recharge when demand or prices are low. The useful reduction depends on the
battery's power rating (kW), which limits how fast it can discharge, and its
state of charge (SOC) and energy capacity (kWh), which limit how long it can
sustain that power.

## State of charge reserve
Operators usually keep a minimum SOC reserve rather than discharging fully.
The reserve protects battery health, keeps backup capacity for outages, and
leaves room to respond to a later event. Discharging below the reserve trades
long-term capability for a short-term reduction.

## Round-trip losses
Charging and discharging lose some energy. Lithium-ion systems commonly return
most, but not all, of the energy put in. Peak shaving is still worthwhile when
the value of reducing demand at peak exceeds the cost of the energy lost.

## EV charging as a flexible load
Electric vehicle charging is often one of the most flexible loads in a building.
Vehicles parked for several hours do not need to charge at full power the whole
time. Deferring or slowing charging during a peak window can cut demand with
little effect on users, provided vehicles still reach their required charge
before departure.

## Managed charging practices
- Set a site-level charging power cap during peak windows
- Prioritise vehicles by departure time or required energy
- Resume charging gradually after the peak to avoid a rebound
- An EV shift only helps if vehicles are actually charging; with no active EV
  load there is nothing to shift
