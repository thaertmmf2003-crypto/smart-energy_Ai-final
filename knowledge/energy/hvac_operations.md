# HVAC operations and load reduction

## Why HVAC dominates building load
Heating, ventilation and air conditioning is usually the largest single energy end
use in commercial and institutional buildings. Surveys of commercial buildings
commonly put space conditioning and ventilation together at roughly a third or
more of total building energy, and the share rises in hot climates and in
buildings with long operating hours. Because HVAC is both large and flexible, it
is normally the first place operators look when demand must come down quickly.

## Why reducing HVAC load reduces building consumption
Chillers, heat pumps, fans and pumps draw electrical power in proportion to the
thermal work they do. Raising the cooling setpoint (or lowering the heating
setpoint) shrinks the temperature difference the plant must hold, so compressors
run at lower capacity or cycle off more often. Fan and pump power falls even
faster than airflow or water flow, because for variable-speed fans and pumps power
scales roughly with the cube of speed. A modest reduction in delivered capacity
can therefore produce a noticeable drop in electrical demand.

## Setpoint adjustment
- A temporary setpoint change of 1 to 2 °C during a peak window is a common
  demand-response measure that most occupants tolerate for short periods.
- Savings per degree depend on climate, envelope, internal gains and plant type.
  Treat rules of thumb as estimates and confirm them against metered data.
- Restore setpoints gradually after the event. A sudden return can create a
  rebound peak as every zone recovers at once.

## Pre-cooling
Cooling a building slightly below its normal setpoint before a forecast peak
stores cooling in the building mass. During the peak the plant can then run at
reduced capacity while indoor temperature drifts back toward the normal setpoint.
Pre-cooling works best in buildings with high thermal mass and predictable
occupancy.

## Occupancy-aware operation
- Spaces with low occupancy can often run with relaxed setpoints and reduced
  ventilation, within the limits of the ventilation standard that applies.
- Laboratories are a special case: fume hoods and pressure relationships often
  require minimum air change rates regardless of occupancy. Do not reduce
  laboratory ventilation without the lab safety officer's agreement.
- Classrooms and offices usually follow a schedule, so HVAC start and stop times
  should track it rather than running around the clock.

## Comfort and safety limits
Load reduction must stay inside comfort and safety envelopes: indoor temperature
bands, humidity limits, minimum outdoor air for occupied spaces, and any
equipment-room cooling requirements (server rooms, freezers, sensitive
instruments). An HVAC action that saves energy but breaches these limits is not
an acceptable action.
